How full-duplex speech models work

A path from "sound is a list of numbers" to fine-tuning a model that listens and speaks at the same time. Written for one project: a speech model that changes its tone to suit the person talking to it.

You need Python and a user's understanding of LLMs: tokens, prompts, fine-tuning as an idea. You do not need any signal processing. Reading this page takes about 20 minutes. The study path at the end takes about 9.5 hours for the short route.

Start here

The whole idea in six sentences

  1. Sound is a very long list of numbers.
  2. A codec compresses it to about 100 tokens per second.
  3. A transformer predicts the next audio token the way an LLM predicts the next word.
  4. The model also writes the words it is about to say, which keeps its language sensible.
  5. Full duplex means the model reads one slice of your audio and writes one slice of its own every 80 milliseconds, with no turns.
  6. Tone lives in the tokens that are easiest to ignore, and teaching a model to use them is this project.

Step 1

Sound is a long list of numbers

A microphone measures air pressure thousands of times a second. Each measurement is a sample. Speech models usually work at 16,000 or 24,000 samples per second, so one second of speech is 24,000 numbers and a ten-second reply is 240,000.

That is the first problem. A text model reads a few tokens per word. If every sample were a token, one sentence would be longer than a book chapter, and the model would spend all its effort on the shape of the wave and none on meaning.

You will also meet the spectrogram: a picture of which pitches are present at each moment. Whisper reads one. It is a useful way to look at audio, but the models in this project do not use it. They use learned tokens.

Step 2

A codec turns sound into tokens

A neural audio codec is two networks trained together. The encoder squeezes audio into a short sequence of vectors. The decoder rebuilds the audio from them. In the middle, each vector is replaced by the closest entry in a fixed list called a codebook, so a slice of sound becomes an integer. That integer is a token.

One codebook is too coarse to sound good. Codecs fix this with residual vector quantization. The first codebook stores its closest match. The second stores the closest match to whatever the first got wrong. The third corrects the second, and so on.

Moshi's codec is called Mimi. It cuts 24,000 samples per second into 12.5 frames per second and writes 8 tokens for each frame. Each token picks one of 2,048 entries. So speech becomes 100 small integers per second, a 240-fold reduction in count.

The eight tokens are not equal. Mimi trains the first one to agree with a speech-understanding model, so it mostly carries what was said. The other seven carry the rest: who is speaking, pitch, loudness, mood, the room. Remember this split. It comes back in step 7.

Step 3

A language model over audio tokens

Once audio is tokens, the LLM recipe applies: train a transformer to predict the next token, then generate by sampling one token after another. Decode the tokens and you hear speech.

There is one snag. Each frame has several tokens, and a transformer step is expensive. Predicting 100 tokens a second in one long row is slow, and the row gets long quickly. Moshi splits the job between two transformers.

The temporal transformer is the big one: 32 layers. It takes one step per frame and reads the whole conversation so far. The depth transformer is small: 6 layers. It takes the big model's summary and writes the tokens of the next frame one at a time. The big model only runs 12.5 times a second, which keeps it fast enough to talk in real time.

Step 4

The model writes what it is about to say

Models that predict only audio tokens speak fluently and reason badly. They sound like speech and drift in meaning. Moshi's fix is called the inner monologue. Alongside its audio, the model predicts a stream of text tokens that is lined up in time with its own speech. The text for a word appears just before the audio for that word, and padding tokens fill the gaps.

In effect the model writes each word and then says it. This keeps the language quality of a text LLM. It also gives us a window: the text stream is something we can read, time-stamp and measure while the model talks.

Step 5

Full duplex: two streams, one clock

A cascade takes turns. It waits for you to stop, thinks, then talks. A full-duplex model has no turns. Time is cut into 80 millisecond frames, and at every frame the model does the same thing: it reads the 8 tokens of your audio for that frame, and writes one text token and 8 tokens of its own audio. Often the audio it writes is silence.

Nothing in the model says "now it is my turn". Starting early, murmuring agreement and stopping when interrupted are all the same act: predicting the next frame given both streams. The model learns when to speak from recordings of real conversations where each person is on a separate channel.

This is also why a full-duplex model can react to how you sound while it is still talking. Your audio keeps arriving, frame after frame, the whole time.

Step 6

How these models are trained

The training signal is the same at every stage: hide the next token, ask the model to predict it, and nudge the weights when it is wrong. What changes is the data.

StageDataWhat the model learnsWho can do it
Text pre-trainingA large text corpusLanguage and knowledgeLabs
Audio pre-trainingAbout 7 million hours of speechTo speak and to hearLabs
Duplex trainingAbout 2,000 hours of two-channel phone calls, then about 20,000 hours of synthetic dialogueTiming, overlap, and an assistant's voice and mannerLabs
Fine-tuningA few hours to a few tens of hours of your own dialoguesOne new behaviourYou, on one rented GPU

The first three rows are the Moshi recipe and are out of reach for one person. The last row is where this project lives.

Fine-tuning starts from the released weights and continues training on a small dataset. LoRA makes that cheap: the original weights are frozen, and small extra matrices are trained beside them. Far fewer numbers change, so it fits on one GPU and the model is less likely to forget what it already knew.

The data for a Moshi fine-tune is plain: stereo audio files with the assistant on the left channel and the user on the right, plus a file of word timestamps for the assistant's side. Silences and overlaps stay exactly where they happened, because the timing is part of what is being taught.

Two things that surprise people

A falling loss does not mean the model got better at conversation. During training the model always sees the true past. When it runs freely it sees its own output, and small errors can compound into silence or babble. Always listen to free-running output.

The model learns exactly what the data rewards. If the right answer never depends on how the user sounded, the model has no reason to listen to that.

Step 7

Why tone is the hard part

Say one sentence in a plain voice, then in a low, slow voice. The words are the same, so the first token row keeps its pattern. Almost everything that changed is in the other seven rows and in the timing.

Three things make this hard for a model.

  • The words are usually enough. In most training data the best reply can be guessed from the words alone, so a model reaches a low loss without using the voice. Researchers call this lexical dominance. The LISTEN paper measured it in six audio models.
  • The right data is rare. A model only learns to use tone from examples where the same words, said differently, get different replies. Ordinary speech corpora and most synthetic dialogues do not contain such pairs.
  • Knowing is not doing. A 2026 study of Moshi found that emotion can be read out of the model's internal activity, yet using that to steer how it speaks only partly worked. A separate 2026 benchmark played whispered requests to seven voice systems, and only 2 of 35 replies came back in a lowered voice.

So the project needs two things that the earlier cascade work already started: paired data where delivery changes the right answer, and a test that holds the words fixed and changes only the voice.

Step 8

What "your own model" can mean

RouteWhat it takesWhat you get
Train a foundation model from scratchMillions of hours of audio and a rack of GPUsNot possible for one person. Rebuilding Moshi would not be new either.
Fine-tune an open full-duplex modelYour own paired data, one rented GPU for a few hours per run, a small budgetYour own weights with a behaviour that came from your data, and an experiment that shows it. This is the main route.
Train a tiny duplex model from scratchA laptop or one GPU, a small corpus, frozen Mimi tokensPoor conversation, excellent understanding. A good learning exercise and a good blog post.

What makes the fine-tune yours is an experiment we did not find in published work: showing that a model learns to respond to how something is said, without being told to, and that the effect disappears when the pairing in the training data is shuffled.

Reference

Words you will meet

Sample rate
How many measurements of the sound wave are stored per second. Mimi uses 24,000.
Frame
One slice of time for the model. In Moshi a frame is 80 milliseconds, so there are 12.5 per second.
Codec
An encoder and decoder that turn audio into tokens and back.
Codebook
A fixed list of vectors. A token is the position of the closest one.
RVQ
Residual vector quantization. Several codebooks in a row, each one encoding the error left by the one before.
Semantic token
The first token of a frame, trained to carry mostly the words.
Acoustic tokens
The remaining tokens of a frame. Voice, pitch, mood and room.
Temporal transformer
The large model that steps once per frame.
Depth transformer
The small model that writes the tokens inside one frame.
Inner monologue
The text stream Moshi writes just ahead of its own speech.
Full duplex
Listening and speaking at the same time, with incoming audio able to change what the model is saying.
Backchannel
A short sound such as "mm-hm" that shows you are listening without taking the turn.
Prosody
The melody and rhythm of speech: pitch movement, stress, pauses, pace, loudness.
Teacher forcing
Training on the true past rather than on the model's own output.
LoRA
Fine-tuning by training small added matrices while the original weights stay frozen.
Lexical dominance
A model answering from the words and ignoring how they were said.
Figures are schematic. The numbers for Moshi and Mimi come from the Moshi paper and from Kyutai's released code.
