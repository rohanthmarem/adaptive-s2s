    does moshi hear tone?

    i played moshi the same sentences in a sad, excited and plain voice, and recorded every layer while it listened

    By Rohanth Marem·11.15.26·
    contents

      the question

      I'm building a voice model that changes how it talks based on how you sound. If you call it sounding wrecked, it should slow down and be gentle. If you call it buzzing about good news, it should match that energy. The first version I built was a cascade: speech to text, an LLM, then text to speech. It works, but it throws your voice away at the very first step, so the best it can do is guess your mood from your words.

      Moshi, from Kyutai, works differently. It's a full-duplex model, which means it listens and talks at the same time, and it never turns your voice into text first. Your audio goes straight in as tokens. So in theory it has everything it needs to notice that you sound sad. In practice, when I played it a sad and an excited version of the same line, it answered them pretty much the same way.

      Before spending money fine-tuning it, I wanted to know which of two things was going on. Either Moshi doesn't pick up tone at all, and I'd have to teach it to hear. Or it hears tone just fine and doesn't do anything with it. Those need very different fixes, so I opened it up and looked.

      the setup

      The test needs sentences where the words give nothing away and only the voice carries the mood. I wrote two scenes like that. In one, someone just got a call back about a job they really wanted: "So, they called me back. About the job." In the other, someone just checked the grade that decides whether they stay in their program: "I just checked my grade for that course." Each line was read three ways: excited, sad and plain.

      My first clips came from Grok's text-to-speech, using a voice called Eve. You control the emotion there with tags like [sigh] or <slow>, and the emotion kept fading partway through. One "sad" clip only sounded sad on two of its four sentences. So I switched to Qwen3-TTS, an open model running locally on my Mac, with one of its built-in voices called Ryan. It takes a written instruction for every sentence, like "sad the whole way through", so I rendered each sentence on its own, made three takes and kept the one an emotion classifier scored best. That gave 333 takes across all the scenes, and 81 for the same-words test (9 sentences, 3 tones, 3 takes).

      Then I played each take into Moshi on its own while forcing Moshi to stay silent. Its text was pinned to the "say nothing" token and its audio to the codes for silence, so the only thing that changed from run to run was the voice coming in. Every 80 ms, I saved what Moshi was holding at the input and after each of its 32 layers.

      can you read tone out of it?

      The simplest way to ask whether a layer "knows" something is to try to read it back out. For every layer I averaged its state over a clip and trained a tiny classifier to guess the tone. The catch is that the classifier never sees the sentence it's tested on. It learns from the other eight sentences and has to name the tone of the ninth, so it can't cheat by recognising the words. With three tones, guessing gets you 33%.

      Tone is readable everywhere. About 79% of clips can be read straight from the input, before any layer has run, which makes sense: the codec keeps what your voice sounds like, so the information is there from the start. Clip length on its own only gets 31%, so this isn't just "sad clips are longer".

      The part I didn't expect is what happens after the speaker stops. For the next 1.2 seconds the input is pure silence, and the input layer drops to 72%. The transformer layers hold 84 to 88% the whole way up to layer 31. Moshi keeps a memory of how you sounded after you've finished talking.

      what lights up

      A quick word on "neurons". The 4096 numbers at each layer aren't neurons in the biological sense. They're the running list that every layer reads and edits, which people call the residual stream. Still, you can ask the same question of each one: does it go up when the voice is sad, and down when it's excited?

      There's no single sad neuron. At the first layer, 759 units shift by more than a full wobble in both the job scene and the grade scene, every one of them in the same direction both times, and none in opposite directions. Hundreds of units each move a little, together. So the useful thing to measure isn't a unit, it's a direction in that 4096-number space.

      watching it listen

      That direction makes the whole thing easy to watch. I took the average "sad" state minus the average "excited" state (after putting every unit on the same scale, so a few huge units don't drown out the rest). Then, at every 80 ms frame, I checked how far Moshi's state sat along that line. Press play and the dot follows the audio.

      The lines split almost as soon as the voice starts and stay apart while it plays. Then, once the voice stops, they drift back together. At first that looked like it contradicted the 84 to 88% from before, so I checked. The direction that separates tones while you're talking and the one that separates them afterwards barely overlap (cosine similarity of 0.14 to 0.42 depending on the layer). Moshi files the memory of your tone somewhere other than where it first heard it.

      the map

      Here's a view that doesn't use the tone labels at all. For each sentence I subtracted its average state, which removes what the words contribute, and then flattened the 4096 numbers down to the two patterns that vary the most. Nobody told the flattening about tone. If the colours still clump, the voice is what moves Moshi most once the words are gone.

      does it carry over?

      A direction learned from two scenes could just be memorising those scenes. So I tested it on stories it never saw. I learned the sad direction from the job and grade calls only, then used it to score a different scene: someone telling you their dog died, read sad and read plain. Same for excited, tested on someone who just got a job offer.

      It transfers almost perfectly for sad and very well for excited. The sad-minus-excited direction from the job scene and the one from the grade scene also point the same way (cosine similarity of 0.85 to 0.91), so this is one shared axis rather than two lucky ones.

      Every one of those clips is still the same Qwen voice, though, which was my biggest worry. So I went back to the old Grok clips: a different engine, a female voice, longer scripts and tag-based emotion. I used the same directions with no refitting.

      It still works. Sad lands past plain and plain lands past excited in both scripts. But the strip shows something more interesting: Grok's flat and hesitant clips land even further toward the sad end than its actual sad clips. So the axis isn't really "sadness". It's closer to energy: bright, fast and animated at one end, low, slow and flat at the other. Psychologists call this arousal, and it's the easiest part of emotion to hear in a voice. Telling sad apart from bored or unsure would need a second direction, and probably training data that separates them.

      the bottleneck

      So Moshi hears tone, holds onto it and represents it in a way that generalises. Does any of that reach what it says?

      Barely. Layer 31 is the last stop before Moshi picks its next word, and tone is readable there 85% of the time. Push that same state through the word head and it drops to around half. Moshi's chance of starting to talk is the same for every tone, about 2.5% per frame, and its top first word is "Yeah" for all three. If I ignore the silence token and only compare word choices, there's a faint fingerprint of tone, but the shift between a sad and an excited clip is barely bigger than the shift between two takes of the same tone.

      The information reaches the doorway and doesn't get used to choose what to say.

      beyond happy and sad

      Sad versus excited is the easiest contrast there is. One is slow and quiet, the other fast and bright. Real people sound sarcastic, bored, disgusted or quietly anxious, and a model that adapts its tone has to tell those apart too. So I widened the test to twelve emotions: neutral, happy, sad, angry, anxious, surprised, disgusted, bored, sarcastic, amused, tender and contemptuous.

      I wrote ten sentences whose words fit any mood ("They moved the meeting to Friday", "So that's what you decided", "You cooked dinner for everyone"), and had each one read in all twelve emotions by four voices: Qwen's Ryan and Serena, plus two voices generated from a written description, a woman in her thirties and a man in his forties. Every line was rendered twice and the better take kept, which gave 480 clips. The renders aren't equally good. An emotion classifier agreed with 35 of 40 neutral clips, 34 happy and 31 angry, but only 24 sad, 8 surprised, 6 anxious and 6 disgusted. Bored, sarcastic, amused, tender and contemptuous have no label in that classifier, so I picked those takes by loudness, pitch movement and pace. Some of what follows is limited by the renders, not by Moshi.

      Then I ran the same probe as before, but harder: it had to name the emotion of a voice it was never trained on.

      Moshi gets it right 38% of the time on a voice it has never heard, against 8% for guessing, and 45% on a sentence it has never heard. It climbs through the first ten layers and peaks around layer 9, then holds. After the voice stops it still carries about a third of it. Not perfect, but real.

      The mistakes are more interesting than the score. Angry (28 of 40), amused (28) and happy (26) are easy. Sarcastic (3), contemptuous (4), anxious (7) and bored (8) mostly aren't. And the confusions follow a pattern: sad and tender get swapped, anxious and bored both drift towards sad, and surprised, sarcastic and contemptuous mostly get heard as happy. They're all mixed up with something at the same energy level.

      Laying the emotions out shows the same thing. Amused sits off on its own along the strongest direction, which makes sense since those clips have actual laughing in them. Everything else lines up along the next one: angry, happy and surprised at one end, sad, tender and bored at the other. When I checked what that direction tracks, it lines up with how high the voice is (a correlation of 0.81 across the twelve emotions) and how much the pitch moves (0.75), and not with loudness at all (0.07). That's the energy axis from the Grok test again, and it's mostly pitch. The probe still gets plenty right within the same pitch range, so there are finer distinctions in there, but this is the biggest one.

      the neurons

      Then the part I was most curious about: are there neurons for any of this? Each of Moshi's 32 layers has 11,264 MLP neurons, 360,448 in total. For every neuron and every emotion I measured how far it moves for that emotion compared with the rest, separately in each of the four voices, and only counted it if it moved by more than a full spread in the same direction in all four. With the emotion labels shuffled, that rule finds zero neurons. With the real labels, it finds 86.

      So yes, but not many, and they're lopsided. 52 of the 86 are for anger and 15 for sadness, and 66 sit in the first eleven layers, close to the input, where they're probably reacting to the raw sound, like pitch, rather than anything you'd call understanding. Sarcasm gets none at all, not even at a looser threshold. Combined with the probe results, the picture is that emotion mostly isn't stored in a few dedicated neurons. It's spread thinly across many of them as a direction, which is what the 4096-number analysis earlier suggested too.

      Every one of these neurons, with the clips that push it furthest and its frame-by-frame trace, is in the neuron atlas. Sad and happy are highlighted throughout so you can compare them directly.

      Sarcasm is the one I don't know how to read yet. It might be that Moshi really doesn't pick it up. But sarcasm usually works through a mismatch between the words and the voice, and these sentences were picked to be neutral, so there was nothing for the voice to contradict. It's also the hardest thing to render, and nothing here checks that the sarcastic clips actually sound sarcastic. That needs real recordings and a sentence that sets it up.

      how this compares

      Two other voice models came up while I was doing this, and they sit on either side of Moshi.

      Dessa, from a small research lab at Block, is full-duplex like Moshi but trained from scratch on nothing but recorded human conversation. No text, no pretrained language model. The page says it "natively hears and speaks emotion, sarcasm, laughter, a frustrated sigh, a voice cracking with sadness", and the demos are full of backchannels, laughs and pace changes. There's no paper, no architecture, no numbers and nothing about what happens inside it, so it's a set of claims for now, not a result I can check. What makes it interesting next to these results is the missing text stream. Moshi speaks by first predicting a word (its "inner monologue") and then the audio for it, and the word head is exactly where the tone signal dies. A model that never routes through text doesn't have that doorway. If Dessa really does sound this responsive, that would be one explanation worth testing.

      Sesame's CSM takes the opposite route. It isn't full-duplex: it takes turns, reading the whole conversation so far as interleaved text and audio, and then generates its reply with a big backbone transformer plus a small audio decoder. It uses the same Mimi codec as Moshi, so it hears your voice through the exact same 8 tokens per 80 ms that already carried 79% of the tone in my test. Their own evaluation is the telling part. Without context, listeners can't tell CSM from a real recording. Given the previous 90 seconds of conversation, they consistently prefer the real person. In their words, "a noticeable gap remains between generated and human prosody in conversational speech generation". That's the same gap I'm poking at from the inside: the tone is in the input, and the model doesn't fully use it to decide how to respond.

      The neuron atlas borrows its shape from Goodfire's neuron atlas for Qwen3.6. Their argument is that MLP neurons, the gated units inside each layer, are more interpretable than people assumed: most have one main job, and they treat each neuron's positive and negative extremes ("poles") separately. Moshi's layers use the same kind of gated neuron, 11,264 per layer, so the same lens fits.

      what it means for the build

      This is good news for the project. The hard part, hearing emotion in a voice, is already there. A fine-tune doesn't have to teach Moshi to listen. It has to connect a signal Moshi already has to the choice of what to say and how to say it. That's a much smaller job, and it's the kind of thing a small LoRA fine-tune should be able to do.

      The next experiment is a causal one. Everything above shows that tone is present; it doesn't show that changing it changes anything. So I'll take the sad direction, add it into Moshi's layers while it hears a plain reading in a live call, and see whether its replies shift. If they do, some of the adaptation might come from steering alone. If not, fine-tuning is the way.

      caveats

      • Every clip is synthetic speech. Two different engines and voices help, but real people sound messier.
      • Each clip is a single sentence played cold, with no conversation before it, and Moshi was held silent the whole time.
      • The word-head numbers come from pushing averaged states through the head, which is a rough approximation of what happens frame by frame.
      • I only looked at the words. Tone might still change how Moshi's own voice sounds, and I haven't measured that yet.
      • The Grok check is two scripts. It's encouraging, not proof.
      • The twelve-emotion clips are rendered speech too, and several emotions (surprised, anxious, disgusted, and probably sarcastic and contemptuous) aren't rendered convincingly. Low scores for those may be the renders' fault rather than Moshi's.
